Section 1
01
Real engagement: Battlbox, CEO John Roman, 6-person team.

The before: a 6-person team that couldn't see where its own time went

Battlbox is a real, delivered Go2 engagement. Before we touched it, the team ran the way most back-office teams run: people moving between tools all day, with no instrumented record of where the hours actually went or which of them were recoverable.

That blind spot is the problem we solve. AI tools only work on what people explicitly hand them. Go2 works from what people actually do. Until you measure the actual work, “where should we automate?” is a guess.

Section 2
02
One capture layer, two purposes: staffing work profile and automation insight engine.

What we installed: the same daemon, watching the real work locally

We installed our telemetry daemon on the team's machines. It captures keystrokes, app usage, URLs, idle time, and workflow patterns locally, plus periodic screenshots interpreted on-device via OCR.

Sensitive data — card numbers, SSNs, passwords — is detected and excluded at the daemon level before it ever touches disk. Every data stream is independently toggleable.

This is not a new product. It closes the loop on a system we already had. The same daemon that built a work profile to match applicants now feeds the automation insight engine.

Section 3
03
Measured from observed work, not estimated from a survey.

What we measured: 5,343 hours, and most of it automatable

Over roughly six months we analyzed 5,343 hours of observed work across the 6 people. The finding: 190–250 hours per month of automatable work — about 80–85% of the observed work.

The report does not hand you a single bravado number. Automation opportunities are ranked by impact with confidence scores, and the recoverable-hours estimate ships with its assumptions stated, so you can audit the math instead of trusting it.

Section 5
05
The report is a hypothesis; post-automation measurement is the proof.

The before/after proof point we care about most

The single most valuable data point in any engagement is the after: leave the daemon on and measure actual time saved against the pre-automation baseline.

That before-and-after measurement is offered as an optional follow-up — the daemon stays on two weeks later to measure real recovered hours. It is the client's call, never automatic.

We'd rather show a measured delta than a projected one.

What that engagement cost us to produce

The comparable engagement in the market: $25K–$50K, 4–8 weeks, and you get a PDF with no implementation. Our cost to generate one audit is about $15 in compute and roughly an hour of processing, with no human labor beyond triggering the analysis.

To be precise: the ~$15 is raw compute to produce one report. The realistic buyer-facing monthly AI/compute figure to plan against is $300–500/mo, not “a few dollars.”

Section 7
07
The conflict in the old model is stated plainly, not hidden.

Why we ran Battlbox at all — the conflict, stated plainly

We were losing $1,200/month on that account as a staffing client. The audit was the clean way to transition the team directly to John and exit staffing on good terms.

The old staffing model had a built-in conflict: selling staffing meant no incentive to automate away our own revenue. By end of 2023 we watched AI start to cannibalize that business, so we pulled it from that stack.

Standalone, sold to the right buyer, that conflict is gone. The audit and the automation are now aligned with the customer instead of against them.

08
Early signal is demo conversion on buyer data, not signed-invoice data.

Is one engagement repeatable? The early signal

Battlbox is one data point. The early repeat signal: in one week we ran 6 demos and 6 of 6 moved forward. That is demo conversion on the prospect's own data, not signed-invoice data.

The reason it converts is there's no theater: every demo runs on the buyer's actual workflow, not a canned example.

Most teams do not act on the insights even when the opportunity is obvious. That's why we don't sell the outcome; we sell the audit and the built tools, and the managed retainer exists to make sure the work actually gets implemented.

Section 9
09
Lead with intelligence first, automation second.

The motion the engagement turns into

The wedge is the audit. Battlbox shows the shape: free 1-person, 2-week pilot as the hook; full team audit; then an implementation retainer to do the building and maintenance.

We are deliberately selling managed SMB pilots now — not launching a self-serve platform — and keeping the core workflow-truth and control plane closed while we prove the market.

There is a proposed pricing stack, but it is explicitly not yet validated. The posture is ship a price, learn, adjust.

Section 10
10
Defensibility starts with the data feed, not the report.

The edge under the engagement: the data feed

The defensible part isn't the report — it's what the report is built from. Go2 starts from the actual work; competitors start from tickets, CRM records, or chat logs.

Think of it as a faster, more granular feed of how work really happens — and the intelligence and automation sit on top of that feed.

The pipeline itself is replicable by a funded competitor in 6–12 months. The durable defensibility is speed to market, the data network effect, implementation lock-in, and a privacy-first, local-first posture — not the code.

Section 11
11
Local-first is the reason the pilot can run on customer machines.

Why this is safe to run at scale — local-first

Data lives in a local database on the worker's machine, protected by that machine's existing security; nothing is uploaded or transmitted during the pilot. Optional end-of-pilot zero-data-retention cloud processing is decided together, never automatic.

The cloud Go2 Desktop is SOC 2 compliant, and the pilot daemon is the same codebase — the difference is the data stays local. The local-only pilot daemon is not separately certified; local data is protected by the machine's existing security, not Go2-added encryption.

The enterprise path: a credible direction, not closed proof

We have been enterprise-ready — that's not the hard part. The deliberate choice is to run the SMB audit wedge now because it makes the IP more valuable, and you can't harden enterprise and run the SMB motion at full speed in the same window.
